The launch-angle era gave hitting a genuinely useful correction. Swinging slightly up at a ball
arriving slightly down is better than chopping at it. That part is right and it is not going away.

The cue that came out of it is still incomplete.

## One number, three dimensions

Attack angle is the vertical component of bat path at contact. It is a real quantity and it matters.
It is also one of three, and the other two decide most of what happens:

- **Horizontal direction** — where the barrel is pointed in the plane. This is most of the
  difference between a pulled ball and one going the other way, and it moves within an at-bat.
- **Bat tilt** — the roll of the barrel. Two hitters at identical attack angle with different tilt
  are not making the same swing and will not miss the same way.

A hitter can hit a target attack angle and still be wrong in both other axes on every swing. The
number on the readout is green. The at-bat is not going well.

## Where the ball is matters more than the average

Attack angle is usually reported as a hitter average. But bat path is not one number a hitter *has*
— it varies through the zone, by pitch height, by depth of contact. Averaging across it produces a
figure that describes no particular swing.

The useful version is a surface, not a scalar: what the path does at the top of the zone versus the
bottom, out front versus deep. Two hitters with the same average can have opposite problems.

## What a whiff is actually telling you

Missing the ball is reported as one event. It is at least three:

- **Under it.** The barrel arrived below the ball.
- **Over it.** Above.
- **Early or late.** The barrel arrived at the right height at the wrong time.

Different failures, different fixes, and a swing-and-miss rate collapses all of them into a single
percentage. A hitter who misses under everything and a hitter who misses over everything have
identical whiff rates and opposite problems, and one instruction — "swing up" — will help exactly
one of them.

## What I say instead

Not "swing up." Something closer to: *arrive on the plane of the pitch you are trying to hit, aimed
where you intend, and be able to do it twice.*

Longer, less catchy, no worse as a cue, and it does not quietly instruct half the hitters in the
room to make their actual problem worse.
