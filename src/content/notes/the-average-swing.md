title: "The Average Swing Doesn't Exist"
summary: 'Averaging a hitter’s reps produces a swing nobody has ever taken, and then we coach it.'

Every swing report I have ever seen opens with a mean. Average bat speed, average attack angle,
average time to contact. It's the natural thing to do with twenty numbers, and it quietly throws
away the most interesting thing in the data.

A mean assumes the twenty swings were twenty attempts at the same thing. They weren't. Some were
2-0 hunting a fastball. Some were 0-2 fighting off a slider away. The hitter changed the swing on
purpose, correctly, and the average of those swings is a swing that was never intended and never
taken. Then we hand it to them on a page and ask why their attack angle is inconsistent.

The version I'd rather see is boring to build and much harder to argue with: report the mean, then
report how much of the spread around it was doing a job. If most of the variation lines up with
what the pitch demanded, the hitter is adjusting and the number is fine. If it doesn't line up with
anything, that's the number to chase.

It matters most for the hitters who look worst on a report. A wide spread reads as
"inconsistent," gets coached as a mechanics problem, and sometimes belongs to the best adjuster in
the room. There's no way to tell those apart from a mean, which means the report can't tell you,
which means we should stop asking it to.
