A group cage block has a failure mode everybody recognizes: eight hitters, one machine, and a lot
of standing around taking comfortable swings at a known pitch. The reps happen. The learning
doesn't, because nothing about the environment resembles the thing the reps are for.

Machine games are the fix I keep coming back to. The structure is simple — turn the round into a
scored game with a constraint that forces the behavior you're actually trying to train, then let
the competition supply the pressure that a coach's voice can't.

## What makes one work

- **The constraint does the coaching.** If you want a hitter to stay through the middle, score the
  middle and stop talking. A rule that makes the wrong swing unprofitable teaches faster than a cue
  that asks for the right one.
- **The score has to be public.** Private scoring is practice. Public scoring is competition, and
  competition is the variable that makes a cage rep resemble an at-bat.
- **Uncertainty is the point.** A machine set to one pitch at one speed is a batting tee with extra
  steps. Mixing speeds, locations, or sequencing — even crudely — is what forces the read instead
  of the rehearsal.
- **Short rounds, real stakes.** The block should end while people still care about the outcome.

## Why I think it transfers

The constraint-led argument is that skill is coupled to the environment it was built in. A swing
grooved against a predictable machine is genuinely excellent at hitting predictable machines. Add
back the uncertainty and the consequence, and you're training the whole task — read, decide,
execute — rather than the last third of it.

This is a coaching concept, written from my own practice. No internal materials, no athlete data.
